Shared genetic architecture of coronary artery disease and rheumatoid arthritis

People with rheumatoid arthritis are more likely to develop heart disease. I wanted to know whether the two diseases share a genetic basis, and what that shared signal is doing at the molecular level.

Type
Independent project
Data
GWAS Catalog, ENCODE, eQTLGen
Genome build
GRCh37 / hg19
Code
GitHub ↗

What I did

I started from two large public GWAS: coronary artery disease (Aragam et al. 2022, over a million people) and rheumatoid arthritis (Ishigaki et al. 2022, five ancestry groups). Filtering both at genome-wide significance (p < 5×10⁻⁸) gave 18,349 CAD and 35,227 RA variants, and 160 of them were significant in both diseases.

I mapped those 160 SNPs to their nearest genes with bedtools, which left 38 SNPs sitting directly inside a gene. Next I asked whether those SNPs fall on "switched-on" DNA. I used H3K27ac ChIP-seq from ENCODE for two cell types: CD4+ T cells, which attack joint tissue in RA, and CD14+ monocytes, which become the cells that build arterial plaque in CAD. 15 SNPs were active in both. Finally, I checked those 15 against eQTLGen (31,684 blood samples) to see which ones actually change how much a gene is expressed.

The whole analysis runs as eight numbered Bash scripts using awk, sort, comm, grep and bedtools, so it can be rerun from scratch and give the same numbers.

What I found

160SNPs shared by both diseases
15active in both immune cell types
3validated eQTLs, all for HLA-C

The shared SNPs piled up in the HLA region on chromosome 6 (HLA-B, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-A), the immune system's control center. By the end of the funnel, all three validated variants regulate the same gene, HLA-C. The strongest, rs9266196, has a Z-score of about 47.6.

Going from 53,000 variants to three that tell the same story suggests the overlap between RA and CAD is a specific immune-regulatory signal, not noise. It points to a shared genetic root, rather than RA simply causing heart disease through years of inflammation.

Limitations and next steps

  • The nearest gene is not always the causal gene. The eQTL step is there to add real evidence, but it doesn't settle it.
  • This is a strong chain of evidence, not proof. Showing causation would take an experiment, such as editing the SNP in cells and measuring HLA-C.
  • I didn't stratify by ancestry. Next I'd test each ancestry group separately and add more cell types and tissues.

Mitochondrial DNA heteroplasmy and preterm birth risk

Heteroplasmy means carrying a mix of slightly different mitochondrial DNA copies in your own cells. The placenta is extremely energy-hungry, so I asked whether this variation in mothers relates to preterm birth or gestational age.

Where
Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Wallace Lab
Data
8,599 mothers, 5 sites in Africa and South Asia
Outcomes
Preterm birth, gestational age
Code
GitHub ↗

What I did

Each mother had 126 heteroplasmy variants measured, plus age and BMI. Because many variants are correlated, I compressed them with PCA into 14 components that keep about 80% of the variation. I tested those components with logistic regression (preterm birth) and linear regression (gestational age), with FDR correction.

The key step was a mixed-effects model. Site turned out to be a huge predictor on its own, with some sites at 2–3 times the preterm-birth rate. A normal regression lets site soak up almost all the explanatory power. Treating site as a random effect separates differences between sites from differences between mothers at the same site, so a smaller biological signal can show through. I also trained a Random Forest with 5-fold cross-validation and used SHAP to see which features it relied on.

What I found

126 → 14variants compressed to PCs
4mtDNA components significant in the mixed model
0.62Random Forest AUC, combined cohort

With site as a regular predictor, the Bangladesh and Pakistan sites showed 2.3–2.6 times higher odds of preterm birth and no mtDNA feature survived correction. With site as a random effect, four mtDNA components (PC1, PC5, PC11 and PC14) came out significant, along with maternal age. Testing all 125 raw variants one at a time didn't find anything the components had missed.

So heteroplasmy carries a real but modest signal. It's one contributing factor, not a standalone predictor, and how you model site decides whether you can see it at all.

Limitations

  • Only 609 preterm cases out of 8,599, so PR-AUC was low (0.088) even though ROC-AUC was 0.62.
  • In the African subcohort alone, AUC fell to 0.50. Part of the combined model's performance comes from learning which cohort a mother is in.

Mitonuclear ancestry mismatch and preterm birth

Mitochondrial and nuclear genes evolved together and need to cooperate. Building on Crawford et al. (2018), I tested whether mothers whose mtDNA lineage doesn't match their population's ancestry have higher preterm-birth risk.

Where
Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Wallace Lab
Data
African and South Asian maternal cohorts
Methods
Chi-square, Fisher's exact, logistic regression
Presented
UD seminar, Feb 2026

What I did

Each mother's mtDNA haplogroup was grouped into one of five ancestral clusters (African, South Asian, East Asian and so on). I then scored whether her lineage matched the ancestry expected for her population. Because this was a direct hypothesis test rather than a prediction task, I used classical statistics: chi-square and Fisher's exact tests for the association, logistic regression for adjusted odds ratios, and Welch's t-test and OLS for gestational age.

What I found

In the African cohort, mothers with the matched African lineage (L) had 69% lower odds of preterm birth than mothers carrying the distant M lineage (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.12–0.81, p = 0.016). A closer mismatch, the E lineage, showed no effect. In the South Asian cohort, where lineages are genetically closer after a long history of mixing, none of the comparisons were significant.

That pattern suggests the effect scales with how far apart the two ancestries are, not just whether they differ. It agrees with the original paper, whose result also replicated in only one of two cohorts, and it extends the question to a population that paper never tested.

Limitations and next steps

  • Only 41 mothers carried the M lineage, so the result needs replication in a larger sample.
  • Socioeconomic factors and healthcare access weren't modeled directly, and they can correlate with ancestry.
  • A continuous, genotype-based ancestry-distance score would sharpen the analysis.

Multi-ancestry HLA variant calling: benchmarking reference genome bias

Does GATK's industry-standard pipeline call variants equally well across ancestries in HLA, the most diverse and most reference-biased part of the genome? HLA drives transplant matching, autoimmune risk and drug reactions, so accuracy gaps there matter clinically.

Status
Pipeline built; compute running on AWS
Samples
12 from 1000 Genomes + 3 GIAB
Region
chr6:28.5–33.5 Mb (GRCh38)
Code
GitHub ↗

Design

The cohort is 12 samples from 1000 Genomes across four ancestry groups (African, European, South Asian and East Asian), plus three Genome in a Bottle samples (HG001, HG002, HG005) that have hand-curated truth sets to score accuracy against. I slice the extended HLA region (chr6:28,510,120–33,480,577) out of each sample's aligned CRAM and run it through the full pipeline, at about 1/500th the size of a whole genome.

Reads are aligned to the full GRCh38 reference with decoy sequences, not an HLA-only reference. HLA genes look so alike that a small reference would misplace reads, which would undermine the benchmark. I use hard filters instead of VQSR because 15 samples is too few for VQSR to train on.

What I expect to find

  • An accuracy gap between European and East Asian genomes. GRCh38 is built mostly from European-ancestry data, so I expect lower precision and recall for HG005 than HG001.
  • A structural gap for African and South Asian genomes. No GIAB truth set exists for these ancestries, so their calls can't be benchmarked at all. That missing resource is a finding in itself.

Where it stands

The full pipeline is written and passes a Snakemake dry run end to end. All reference data and all 15 samples are downloaded and indexed. Alignment needs more memory than my laptop has, so I'm moving the compute-heavy stages to AWS with Docker. There are no results yet.

Molecular classification of leukemia from gene expression

ALL and AML look alike under the microscope but need very different treatment. In 1999, Golub and colleagues showed they could be told apart from gene expression alone. I reproduced that result with modern machine learning and added SHAP to see which genes drive the predictions.

Type
Course final project
Data
Golub et al. 1999 (Kaggle), 72 patients
Features
7,129 genes (Affymetrix)
Split
38 train / 34 test, as in the original

What I did

I log₂-transformed the expression values, kept the 5,000 most variable genes and standard-scaled them. Every preprocessing step was fit on the training set only, to avoid leakage. PCA, UMAP and t-SNE all showed ALL and AML as clearly separate groups before any labels were used.

I trained five classifiers covering a range of complexity: L2 logistic regression, Random Forest, an RBF-kernel SVM, XGBoost and a shallow neural network. I compared them with stratified 5-fold cross-validation, then evaluated each one once on the untouched test set. Finally, I ran SHAP on XGBoost to rank genes by their contribution.

What I found

94.1%test accuracy, logistic regression and XGBoost
> 0.96AUC for every model
3 of 5top SHAP genes also in Golub's signature

Logistic regression and XGBoost each misclassified only two of 34 patients (F1 ≈ 0.93, AUC ≈ 0.98). Logistic regression was the weakest model in cross-validation, because each fold had too few samples, but it tied for best once trained on all 38. Random Forest ranked every patient correctly (AUC 1.00), but the default 0.5 threshold cost it five AML cases.

The top SHAP genes were ZYX, FAH, CST3, CHRNA7 and PTX3. Zyxin was also the highest-weighted gene in Golub's hand-picked 50-gene signature, and FAH and CST3 appear in it too. So the model is relying on biologically meaningful genes. Golub reported about 85% accuracy and declined to predict five uncertain cases; the best models here reach 94% with no abstentions.

Limitations

  • With 34 test patients, these accuracy figures carry real statistical uncertainty.
  • A clinical tool would need to flag cases it's unsure about for review, rather than always returning ALL or AML.

Tissue-specific complement gene expression: liver vs. lymph node

I tested whether the liver is the main production site for the terminal complement components (C8A, C8B, C9) and the factor H-related regulators (CFHR2–5). Lymph node was the comparison because it's where complement gets used rather than made.

Type
Course project
Data
Human Protein Atlas, 3 liver + 3 lymph node
Reads
Single-end bulk RNA-seq
Compute
BioMix HPC cluster

What I did

I ran the full pipeline on the cluster as batch jobs, one script per stage with a loop over the six samples: FastQC for quality control, TrimGalore for trimming, alignment with both STAR and HISAT2 as a cross-check, and featureCounts for quantification. Both aligners mapped over 95% of reads.

For differential expression I used both DESeq2 and edgeR, since they normalize and test differently and agreement between them is reassuring. DESeq2 gave the final significance calls, and edgeR's top 200 genes went into Enrichr for pathway enrichment and STRING with MCODE for network analysis.

What I found

98%of variance on PC1, separating the tissues
7 of 7complement genes higher in liver
3.25×10⁻²³adj. p, complement and coagulation cascades

All seven complement genes were significantly higher in liver. The stronger evidence came without looking for them: complement and coagulation cascades was the single most enriched KEGG pathway (22 of 85 genes). In STRING, all seven genes formed one MCODE cluster in which every gene connects to all six others. The top differentially expressed genes overall were classic liver markers: APOH, ALDOB, FGG, APOB and PLG.

Limitations

  • Three samples per tissue is small. The effect is large enough to survive that, but more replicates would help.
  • This is bulk tissue. Liver is mostly hepatocytes and lymph node mostly lymphocytes, so I can't separate regulation from cell composition. Single-cell or single-nucleus data would.
  • Some log₂ fold changes near −16 reflect genes that are essentially absent in lymph node, not a smoothly measured effect.
  • The finding confirms known biology. The real aim was to build and validate the pipeline, and the biology was the check that it worked.

Natural thyroid peroxidase inhibitors for hyperthyroidism

Drugs like methimazole treat hyperthyroidism by inhibiting thyroid peroxidase (TPO), but they carry real toxicity. I screened natural flavonoids as potentially safer alternatives.

Target
TPO, UniProt P07202 (res. 142–738)
Ligands
4 flavonoids, 4 FDA-approved drugs
Simulation
10 ns MD, 300 K
Code
GitHub ↗

What I did

I built a homology model of TPO's catalytic domain with SwissModel and validated it (ProSA Z-score −10.57, over 90% of residues in favored Ramachandran regions). I docked four flavonoids (Rutin, Kaempferol, Mearnsitrin and Rosmarinic acid) and four approved drugs with CB-Dock and AutoDock Vina. The top complexes went through 10 ns molecular dynamics in Desmond, and I predicted toxicity and drug-likeness with ProTox 3.0 and SwissADME.

What I found

−9.6kcal/mol for Rutin vs. −3.6 for methimazole
≈ 3 ÅRMSD, stable over 10 ns
5000 vs 220predicted LD50, mg/kg

Every flavonoid bound TPO two to three times more strongly than every drug. Rutin was the lead candidate: the strongest binder, a stable complex across the simulation, and a far safer predicted toxicity profile than methimazole.

Limitations

This is an in-silico study. Binding energies and toxicity are predictions, so the results generate hypotheses and would need lab validation.

Clinical and socioeconomic predictors of childhood asthma

Asthma is one of the most common chronic diseases in U.S. children. Using national survey data, I asked which factors are associated with an asthma diagnosis, and whether simple, interpretable models do as well as machine learning.

Data
2017 National Survey of Children's Health
Sample
~21,600 children aged 0–17
Outcome
Lifetime asthma diagnosis (~12%)
Code
GitHub ↗

What I did

I picked 21 predictors across four levels: the child (age, sex, race, BMI class, allergies, breathing difficulties), the family (poverty ratio, household size, insurance, tobacco use, food security, mold), the community (metro status, neighborhood conditions, safety) and behavior (physical activity, sleep, screen time).

I compared three models on a 70/30 train/test split: multivariable logistic regression as an interpretable baseline, LASSO with the one-standard-error rule for a more conservative model, and Random Forest for non-linear effects. I also ran 5-fold cross-validation and checked calibration. Everything was done in R.

What I found

0.838AUC, LASSO (best)
0.8495-fold cross-validated AUC
~1 in 8children with an asthma diagnosis

LASSO and logistic regression slightly outperformed Random Forest, which suggests the signal is mostly additive. That's good news for public health, where interpretable models are easier to act on. Across all three models, recent breathing difficulties was the strongest predictor by a wide margin, followed by allergies and child age. Family poverty ratio ranked second in the Random Forest, so socioeconomic conditions matter beyond clinical symptoms.

ICU cohort characterization and mortality prediction in MIMIC-III

With three classmates, I reproduced a published MIMIC-III study (Dai et al., PLOS ONE 2020) on the MIMIC-III demo database, then extended it with a model to predict in-hospital mortality.

Type
Team project, 4 members
Data
MIMIC-III demo (~100 patients)
Benchmark
Dai et al. 2020, 38,156 patients
Tools
SQL, R

What we did

We joined five MIMIC-III tables (patients, admissions, ICU stays, diagnoses and the ICD-9 dictionary) with SQL to describe the cohort by age, length of stay, care unit and diagnosis, following the paper's methods. We then fit a logistic regression for in-hospital mortality in R and evaluated it with a confusion matrix and ROC analysis.

What we found

0.78ROC-AUC, in-hospital mortality
12% vs 15%in-hospital mortality, demo vs. full
~2 daystypical ICU stay in both

Broad patterns held up in the small demo: septicemia stayed near the top of the diagnoses and a typical ICU stay was about two days. Exact percentages shifted noticeably with only about 100 patients. Common conditions like chronic ischemic heart disease and acute MI were underrepresented, and the mean and median definitions made some differences look bigger than they are.

Takeaway

A demo dataset is good for building and checking a workflow, but precise benchmarks need the full cohort.
